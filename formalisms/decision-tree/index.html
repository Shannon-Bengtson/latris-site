The methods, their references and their limits

Decision trees and the value of information

Used for: rolling back expected values to the optimal strategy; the expected value of perfect information for each uncertainty (grouping chance nodes that resolve the same uncertainty under different choices) and for all of them together; the probabilities at which the best first choice changes.

Limits: Risk-neutral (expected value, no utility function); point probabilities and payoffs (ranges on them go through the probabilistic sensitivity analysis); EVPI needs an uncertainty to have the same probabilities whatever is chosen, and treats separate uncertainties as independent; no imperfect information (EVSI); thresholds for two-state uncertainties only.

References: Raiffa (1968), Decision Analysis; Clemen & Reilly (2014), Making Hard Decisions, 3rd ed.; Howard (1966), "Information Value Theory", IEEE Trans. Systems Science and Cybernetics 2(1)

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis, acceptability curves and the value of information

Used for: sampling every decision-tree payoff, branch value and probability, or Markov transition, cost and utility given a range (beta and gamma by mean and sd, Dirichlet sets of probabilities), seeded Latin hypercube; how often each choice is best; Markov acceptability curves and the expected value of perfect information over a willingness-to-pay grid; the partial EVPI of each input from the same runs (sorted bins for one value, regression for a set); rank correlations between ranges induced by Iman–Conover reordering; Spearman rank correlation for what drives the decision.

Limits: Correlations are rank correlations between single values (not within or with a Dirichlet set); partial EVPI is estimated from the PSA runs (a little high with few runs per bin), one input at a time, no sample information (EVSI); probabilities of leaving a Markov state sampled separately are scaled down when they sum past 1 (the report counts the runs).

References: Briggs, Claxton & Sculpher (2006), Decision Modelling for Health Economic Evaluation, ch. 4–7; Fenwick, Claxton & Sculpher (2001), "Representing uncertainty: the role of cost-effectiveness acceptability curves", Health Economics 10(8); Claxton (1999), "The irrelevance of inference", Journal of Health Economics 18(3); Strong & Oakley (2013), Medical Decision Making 33(6); Strong, Oakley & Brennan (2014), Medical Decision Making 34(3); Iman & Conover (1982), Communications in Statistics, Simulation and Computation 11(3); McKay, Beckman & Conover (1979), Technometrics 21(2)

Global sensitivity analysis: Sobol indices and Morris screening

Used for: apportioning the variance of a result among every input with a range, varied together over their distributions: first-order and total Sobol indices (Saltelli and Jansen estimators on a scrambled Sobol sequence, bootstrap intervals), or Morris elementary effects (μ*, σ) as a cheaper screen.

Limits: Inputs are independent (correlations between ranges are ignored here); a set of probabilities (a Dirichlet: a chance node's branches, a state's transitions out, a Bayesian-network table) is one input, indexed as a group; indices are estimates, with heavy-tailed inputs or few samples they can stray below 0 or above 1 (the intervals say how far to trust them); Morris effects are per unit of an input's quantile, comparable within one result only.

References: Saltelli et al. (2008), Global Sensitivity Analysis: The Primer; Sobol (2001), Mathematics and Computers in Simulation 55; Saltelli et al. (2010), Computer Physics Communications 181(2); Jansen (1999), Computer Physics Communications 117; Morris (1991), Technometrics 33(2); Campolongo, Cariboni & Saltelli (2007), Environmental Modelling & Software 22(10)
