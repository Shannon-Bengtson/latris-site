Which model should you build?

Latris's five formalisms, what each one is for, and how to choose — the reasoning behind the "Formalise" step in the app.

Short answer: start with a causal loop diagram. It is the cheapest honest answer, and every other formalism builds on it. Add a DAG when someone will ask "can we actually estimate that?"; quantify the same diagram as a fuzzy cognitive map or a Bayesian network when magnitudes or probabilities matter; go to full system dynamics when the behaviour over time is the question. You never redraw — the same factors, links and evidence carry across.

One model, five levels of commitment

The five formalisms are not five apps. They're five answers to the same kind of question — what causes what here, and what happens if we act? — at five levels of commitment to that answer. All five are made of the same things: factors and links, where every link is a claim that carries its sources, for and against. What differs is how much you claim:

  • A causal loop diagram keeps every claim qualitative — direction only.
  • A causal DAG asks the estimation question: what would have to be measured before an effect could be separated from confounding?
  • A fuzzy cognitive map adds soft magnitudes — "strongly" or "weakly" — and simulates the tendency of the mechanism.
  • A Bayesian network puts probabilities on discrete states and answers "how likely, given what we know?"
  • A system dynamics model commits fully: stocks, flows, units, and numbers you'd defend in front of a reviewer — to simulate how the system behaves over time.

The evidence discipline is identical at every level: a link without a source is a visible gap, a source that argues against the link stays on the record, and the report says which methods were used and what they can't do. That's what makes any of them defensible — and it's why the choice of formalism is a choice about how much you can commit to, not about which drawing tool to open.

The five, by the question they answer

Causal loop diagram (CLD)

"How does this work? What feeds back on what?"

  • Reach for it when: you're aligning people on the mechanism, hunting for feedback loops and leverage points, or scoping a mess before any data work.
  • You'll need: argued links — each arrow a claim someone will defend, with a source where one exists.
  • What you get: the structure of the problem: loops, polarity, where an intervention would bite. No numbers.
  • Honest limit: arrows are argued, not estimated. A CLD shows where an effect runs, never how large it is.

Causal DAG

"Can that effect even be estimated — and from what data?"

  • Reach for it when: you have (or are about to collect) observational data and someone will ask whether the estimate is confounded.
  • You'll need: a graph you can defend — every edge a causal claim — plus clarity about what could be measured.
  • What you get: an identification strategy: d-separation checks and valid adjustment sets — what to control for, or a statement that no small set suffices.
  • Honest limit: Latris answers identification, not estimation. It will not produce an effect size or a counterfactual; it tells you what data would have to exist for someone to.

Bayesian network (BN)

"How likely is each outcome — and what would change my mind?"

  • Reach for it when: the question is decision-making under uncertainty over discrete conditions — pass/fail, present/absent, low/medium/high — and evidence arrives piece by piece.
  • You'll need: states for each factor and conditional probability tables — elicited from experts, learned from data, or mixed (Latris keeps the provenance of every cell).
  • What you get: exact posterior probabilities: how the picture shifts as evidence comes in, which unknowns matter most, one-way sensitivity with an audit overlay.
  • Honest limit: discrete states and exact inference on modest networks. If your variables are continuous and tightly coupled over time, system dynamics is the better fit.

Fuzzy cognitive map (FCM)

"Which forces dominate — roughly?"

  • Reach for it when: you want more than arrows but can't defend full equations — expert judgement in the form "this pushes hard, that resists weakly".
  • You'll need: signed, weighted links (in [−1, +1]) and, for scenarios, which factors to clamp.
  • What you get: steady-state tendencies under interventions — direction and rough strength of the net effect.
  • Honest limit: semi-quantitative by design. It says which way the water flows and how fast roughly, not what happens in year three.

System dynamics (SD)

"How will this behave over time if we intervene?"

  • Reach for it when: the behaviour over time is the question — accumulation, delays, tipping points, capacity trade-offs — and you're prepared to defend equations and parameters.
  • You'll need: stocks and flows with units that check out, scenario definitions, and — for uncertainty — which parameters are uncertain by how much.
  • What you get: quantitative trajectories under scenarios, uncertainty bands, which feedback loop dominates when, and charts a board can interrogate.
