Fuzzy cognitive maps

Which forces dominate, roughly.

A fuzzy cognitive map adds signed weights to a causal diagram and iterates them to see where the system tends to settle. It's the middle ground between arrows alone and full equations, and a workhorse of participatory modelling. Latris implements Kosko's original formulation and is explicit about its sensitivities.

At a glance

An arrow claimsThe cause pushes the effect up (positive weight) or down (negative weight), with a strength between −1 and +1.
NumbersOne weight per link, usually elicited from people who know the system.
OutputActivation levels between 0 and 1 at the state the iteration settles into: relative tendencies, not quantities with units.
Causal standingA semi-quantitative simulation of the claimed mechanism. Scenarios intervene on the model, not on the world.
In LatrisKosko's update rule with sigmoid, rescaled tanh or step transfer functions; clamped scenario factors; non-convergence flagged.

Where it comes from

Kosko (1986) introduced fuzzy cognitive maps as an extension of the cognitive maps Axelrod (1976) used to represent decision-makers' causal beliefs: signed graphs became weighted ones that could be iterated. In ecology and natural-resource management, Özesmi and Özesmi (2004) set out a multi-step method for building them from stakeholders' knowledge, and fuzzy cognitive mapping is now a common tool in participatory modelling.

Latris uses Kosko's update rule: each factor's next activation is the transfer function applied to the weighted sum of the others' current activations, Aj(t+1) = f(Σi Ai(t) · Wij), iterated until no activation changes by more than a threshold. Variants that add each factor's previous activation to the sum are not offered.

What practitioners know to watch for

The known problemWhat Latris does
Results depend on the transfer function. The function and its steepness (λ) can change where the map settles. The transfer function, steepness, threshold and iteration cap are settings stored with the model, not hidden defaults, so a reviewer can see and rerun them.
Not every map settles. Iteration can oscillate or wander instead of reaching a fixed point. A run that doesn't converge within the cap is flagged as not converged, so it can't pass for a steady state.
The start often doesn't matter. With a sigmoid and this update rule, the steady state is often independent of the initial activations. Scenarios work by clamping factors at fixed activations. Read a clamped run against an unclamped one: the difference is the scenario's effect.
Weights are judgements. They come from people, and different people weight the same link differently. Weights sit on links that already carry a reason, a confidence and sources for and against, and disagreement can be recorded as a challenge with its outcome.
Activations read as quantities. A steady-state activation of 0.8 isn't 80% of anything. Activations are bounded between 0 and 1 by the transfer function, so read them as relative tendencies. When quantities and timing are the question, give the same model stocks and flows as system dynamics.

What Latris implements

  • Inference: Kosko's weighted-matrix iteration to a fixed point, with a convergence threshold and iteration cap.
  • Transfer functions: sigmoid (the default), tanh rescaled to [0, 1], and step, with adjustable steepness.
  • Scenarios: factors clamped at chosen activations for the whole run.
  • Weights validated to the range −1 to +1, set on the links of an existing causal loop diagram without redrawing it.

What it doesn't do

  • Fixed-point analysis only. Limit cycles and chaotic behaviour are not detected or classified, only flagged as non-convergence.
  • No aggregation of several participants' maps into one, and no learning of weights from data.
  • No self-memory (modified-Kosko) update rule.

On the ladder of causation

A fuzzy cognitive map simulates the mechanism you claimed. A clamped scenario says what the model does under an assumed change, not what the world would do, and the methods section of a Latris report says so.
